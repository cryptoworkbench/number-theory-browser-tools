Shor's Algorithm

Shor's algorithm is the reason RSA is considered mortal: given a real quantum computer, it factors N in time polynomial in log N. There is no quantum computer here, so this page swaps the one genuinely quantum step for an honest classical stand-in and says so at every turn.

•

How this works

Shor's algorithm has five stages: classical pre-checks (peel off an even factor, spot a perfect power, or recognize a prime outright), pick a base a and take its gcd with N, find the order of a mod N — the one truly quantum step, done here on a classical computer instead — a parity and root check on that order, and finally gcd post-processing that turns the order into two real factors of N.

Order finding is normally the quantum part: real hardware prepares a superposition, evaluates a^x mod N on every exponent x at once, and reads off the period with quantum phase estimation and an inverse quantum Fourier transform (QFT). This page cannot do that, so it walks a^1, a^2, a^3, ... mod N one multiplication at a time instead — exactly the step real Shor's never has to do classically, and the reason N stays small here.

Verdict

What the quantum part really does

A real quantum computer prepares a superposition over every candidate exponent x at once, evaluates a^x mod N on all of them in parallel, and then applies an inverse quantum Fourier transform (QFT) to turn the hidden period of that function into a measurable peak. Quantum phase estimation reads that peak, and a continued fraction expansion of the measured phase recovers the exact order r — all at a cost polynomial in log N.

This page cannot do any of that. It instead walks the cycle a^1, a^2, a^3, ... mod N one multiplication at a time on an ordinary classical processor — the one thing real quantum hardware never has to do.

Why N stops at 4096

The classical cycle walk above costs up to N modular multiplications per base attempt. A faithful simulation of the quantum register a real device would use needs about N^2 amplitudes — roughly 16,777,216 (16.8 million) at N = 4096. Both of those grow with N itself, while real Shor's algorithm on real quantum hardware grows only with log N. N is capped here at 4096 because there is no quantum computer on this page — not because the arithmetic is hard.
